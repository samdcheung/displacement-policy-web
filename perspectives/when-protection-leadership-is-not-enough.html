When Protection Leadership Is Not Enough

Protection leadership matters. But achieving protection outcomes also requires understanding the political and institutional systems through which change has to occur.

Samuel Cheung

Founder and Director, Displacement Policy

June 2026 · Governance · 4 min read

The gap between protection policy and protection outcomes has long been one of the central challenges in displacement settings. International norms are well developed. Protection responsibilities have become clearer. Coordination arrangements have been strengthened through successive humanitarian reforms. Yet the existence of standards, mandates and structures has never guaranteed that displaced people are protected in practice.

Part of the difficulty is inherent in protection itself. Humanitarian organizations can often deliver assistance directly. Protection outcomes, by contrast, frequently depend on the behaviour and decisions of others: whether authorities prevent forced return, permit freedom of movement, issue documentation, address violence against civilians, allow access to land and livelihoods, or include displaced populations within public services.

Protection therefore depends not only on technical expertise, legal standards or effective coordination, but on the ability to influence the political and institutional environment in which these decisions are made.

Protection Leadership: The Role of UNHCR in Humanitarian Crises, which I helped develop and draft, sought to make this more explicit. It argued that protection outcomes depend on leadership: the capacity to exercise judgement, build coalitions, sustain principled advocacy and shape institutional behaviour in environments where protection risks are politically and operationally contested.

That remains fundamental. But protection leadership itself operates within a wider institutional landscape.

Many of the decisions that determine whether displaced people are protected sit outside the direct authority of humanitarian leaders. Governments determine legal status and freedom of movement. Ministries establish eligibility for education, health care and social protection. Local authorities influence access to land, documentation and services. Development actors may support the public institutions through which longer-term inclusion becomes possible. Different institutions hold different pieces of the protection outcome.

The unresolved frontier for protection leadership is therefore a deeper form of political analysis.

This needs to go beyond identifying stakeholders and mapping their positions. It requires understanding the institutional design and public systems within which protection outcomes are produced: how mandates are assigned, where budgets are decided, how eligibility is defined, what administrative categories determine access, how data systems recognize or fail to recognize displaced populations, how planning cycles constrain action, and what political incentives shape the behaviour of different institutions.

These arrangements matter because exclusion is not always the consequence of weak institutions or a lack of protection awareness. It may be the predictable result of how functioning institutions are designed.

A ministry may be reluctant to assume responsibility for a population that falls outside its statutory mandate. A public programme may exclude displaced people because eligibility is linked to citizenship, residence or a particular administrative category. An emergency response may provide support while a crisis declaration remains in force, only for responsibility to contract when ordinary budget and programme rules resume. A local authority may face the costs of receiving displaced populations without corresponding resources or formal responsibility.

Taken together, these arrangements can create a political economy of exclusion. Protection remains exceptional because the systems through which it would become predictable are organized around bounded mandates, liabilities and populations.

This is where protection leadership has to look beyond normative advocacy.

The question is not only whether a government should provide documentation, permit movement or extend access to a public programme. Leadership must also ask where the incentives that sustain the existing position are produced, where responsibility has been assigned, where resources are controlled, and how administrative rules translate political caution into exclusionary practice.

Understanding these dynamics changes the protection conversation. Instead of treating an institutional barrier simply as something to advocate against, political analysis asks why the barrier exists, what purpose it serves, which actors have an interest in maintaining it, and under what conditions a different arrangement might become possible.

The next requirement is a vision of where the system can go.

Protection standards establish the normative direction. But they do not themselves provide a political or institutional pathway from the current position to a better one.

In practice, protection leaders need to ask not only what government should do, but what a materially better institutional arrangement would look like and how government could realistically move toward it.

This is not about lowering protection standards to what is immediately politically acceptable. Nor is it simply the identification of a compromise. It is about giving institutional form to a protection objective.

If refugees are excluded from health care, for example, the objective may not end with advocacy for access. What administrative arrangement could provide that access? Which existing public system could accommodate it? What documentation would be required? Which ministry would carry responsibility? What concerns about cost, precedent or legal status would have to be addressed? Could access initially be created through a particular population, geography or administrative mechanism and subsequently widened?

The same logic applies across protection problems. The pathway may be from detention toward temporary documentation; from parallel humanitarian provision toward access to a public service; from an informal arrangement dependent on individual officials toward a regular administrative practice; or from repeated emergency response toward a government system capable of addressing displacement over time.

The protection vision is therefore not only a statement of the desired outcome. It is a view of how the relevant institutions could function differently.

Once that direction is clearer, leadership becomes the work of building the actors needed to move toward it.

Different stakeholders hold different forms of authority, capability and influence. A ministry may control the relevant policy while another possesses the operational capacity to implement it. Local authorities may be able to demonstrate an approach before it is adopted nationally. Civil society and displaced communities can bring evidence, legitimacy and pressure. Humanitarian actors bring protection analysis, operational experience and political relationships. Development institutions may support changes to the public systems through which protection can be sustained.

In some situations, financing institutions may also affect what governments consider possible. But protection leadership does not require humanitarian leaders to become public finance specialists. It requires them to understand enough about where decisions are made, where constraints arise and where leverage sits to bring the relevant actors around a credible protection outcome.

This is more than coordination.

Coordination generally begins with the actors already assembled around a humanitarian response. Protection leadership may require identifying actors outside that architecture because they control the policy, institution or public system on which the protection outcome ultimately depends.

The practical progression is therefore from protection analysis to political analysis, from political analysis to an institutional vision, and from that vision to a coalition capable of producing change.

This also helps clarify what protection leadership can and cannot achieve.

Leadership cannot substitute for governmental authority, rewrite institutional mandates or create public systems by itself. Nor can it remove the political constraints within which governments make decisions about displaced populations. But it can determine whether those constraints are simply accepted as the operating environment or analysed as part of the protection problem itself.

The continued challenge for protection leadership is therefore not simply to strengthen advocacy or coordination. It is to understand the institutions through which protection must ultimately be realized, identify how those institutions could move toward a better outcome, and build the political and institutional relationships required to make that movement possible.

Protection leadership may not be enough on its own. But without that combination of political analysis, vision and coalition-building, the gap between protection commitments and protection outcomes is likely to remain exactly where it has been for too long.
