Displacement and the Future of Development Finance

Reflections from the 2026 World Bank–IMF Annual Meetings

Samuel Cheung | Founder & Director, Displacement Policy

Download PDF

The 2026 World Bank–IMF Annual Meetings in Bangkok focused on how countries can create jobs, manage debt, respond to climate risks and attract investment at a time of growing pressure on public finances. The decisions governments and development banks make in these areas also shape how countries respond to displacement.

For those working on refugees, internal displacement and disaster displacement, the implications are significant: whether refugee inclusion can continue after special financing ends, whether public budgets adjust when people move, and whether emerging approaches to climate and development finance can help meet those needs. Drawing on discussions during the Meetings and wider exchanges on development finance, eight reflections consider what the changing development finance agenda means for displacement policy and practice.

1. Humanitarian need does not automatically create sovereign demand

Development finance is organized around government priorities: jobs, growth, investment, macroeconomic stability and public services. Governments borrow, undertake reforms or commit recurrent expenditure when they see sufficient public benefit. Humanitarian need alone does not constitute a sovereign financing proposition—even when favorable lending terms are offered. Rights and protection remain fundamental obligations, while sovereign incentives and fiscal constraints shape how governments finance and deliver them. Inclusion therefore needs to connect with development objectives that governments can own and sustain. A jobs-led agenda, for example, requires work rights, documentation and effective access to employment and services if displaced people are to participate. Expanding investment in infrastructure, critical minerals and economic corridors also creates risks of development-induced displacement, making safeguards, resettlement obligations and their longer-term fiscal consequences relevant from project design onward.

2. A financing window can open the door. What keeps it open?

Dedicated concessional finance for refugee-hosting countries, including the World Bank’s Window for Host Communities and Refugees (WHR), has created incentives for inclusion and policy reform. The question is what endures as allocations, priorities and replenishment cycles change. Do teachers, clinics, documentation systems and municipal obligations enter ordinary mandates and budgets? Are policy commitments maintained when individual projects close? As development finance shifts toward country-led compacts and sector programs, the WHR experience raises a further test: can inclusion be embedded in mainstream programs while retaining the reform incentives, accountability and international cost-sharing that made dedicated financing effective? In severely constrained countries, continuity cannot simply mean transferring costs to host governments: lasting inclusion may require sustained grants or concessional support alongside domestic budgeting. Success depends on both the public capacity created and credible arrangements for financing it over time.

3. Debt sustainability is also a protection issue

Debt distress constrains development choices beyond the immediate burden of debt service. Fiscal consolidation can restrict public investment, social expenditure and the institutional capacity needed to restore growth and withstand shocks. Minimum social-spending floors matter, but do not alone ensure functioning services or sufficient productive investment. For countries managing displacement, population movement creates additional demands on public systems, often when fiscal space is already limited. Discussions around the Low-Income Country Debt Sustainability Framework (LIC-DSF) increasingly raise questions about how credible adaptation investments might reduce future losses, protect economic output and improve fiscal trajectories. These benefits must be demonstrated. A displacement-sensitive fiscal assessment should also identify relocation costs, shifts in local service demand, lost revenues and recurrent obligations that macroeconomic projections may overlook. Grants, concessional terms and, in some circumstances, debt treatment can be essential to a viable financing response.

4. When people move, fiscal responsibility moves too—often without the money

Displacement changes where and when public expenditure is needed. Receiving districts may require classrooms, health workers, housing, water and sanitation before fiscal transfers or staffing formulas adjust, while areas of origin lose revenue yet retain responsibilities. This mismatch is especially acute for internal displacement, which may not activate any special financing window. Climate-finance discussions illustrated increasingly sophisticated ways to map hazards and vulnerability, prioritize investment and track public expenditure. Tracking expenditure is only part of the task: financing systems must also show who benefits, where resources are delivered and whether allocations adjust to changing population needs. A useful fiscal diagnostic therefore follows each responsibility: who delivers which service, under what mandate, to whom, for how long, and with what resources?

5. Disaster-risk financing can buy time

Disaster-risk financing is expanding beyond reliance on appeals and budget reallocations after a shock. Regional risk pools, parametric insurance and contingent credit can provide faster and more predictable financing when disasters strike. These innovations offer important opportunities to strengthen financial preparedness. For displacement, the challenge is to connect these mechanisms with anticipated population movements and the responsibilities of receiving communities. Payout triggers, contingency plans and disbursement channels need to reflect where people may move and which public systems will face additional demands. Rapid liquidity is valuable, but sustained access to housing, education and other services requires complementary financing. Risk reduction, immediate response, recovery and continuing public responsibilities remain distinct financing tasks, including for losses and obligations that cannot be insured.

6. Turning displacement expenditure into credible financing programs

Existing sustainable-finance principles offer a practical way to show what displacement-related public spending will finance, how projects are selected and what results are expected. The International Capital Market Association’s (ICMA) Social Bond Principles already recognize displaced people among potential target populations, while setting out disciplines for project selection, use of proceeds and reporting. They could help governments and development institutions structure and, where appropriate, aggregate eligible expenditure into credible financing programs without inventing a new displacement-specific bond standard. This requires identifying public obligations and investments, costing them, establishing results and deciding which activities belong together. Infrastructure, housing and livelihoods investments may fit social or sustainability financing frameworks; recurrent services, institutional reform and fiscal transfers may require budgets, grants or concessional finance. A label does not itself create fiscal space or make expenditure investable. The limits of private capital are also important: while World Bank Group private-capital mobilization reached $112 billion in FY2026, only around $3 billion was mobilized in low-income countries. Public and concessional finance therefore remain central to many displacement-related obligations. Issuer capacity, financing terms and debt sustainability remain decisive. The instrument should follow the financing proposition.

7. Social protection can strengthen resilience and sustained inclusion

Social protection is increasingly recognized as an investment in resilience and inclusive growth. Registries, payment systems and contingency arrangements enable governments to expand assistance during shocks, while protecting social expenditure within fiscal programs and medium-term budgets can help sustain essential capacity. These developments offer a route for including displaced populations in ordinary public systems. Eligibility, documentation and portability matter as households cross jurisdictions, as do outreach, referrals, case management and effective links to education and health services. The benefits extend beyond immediate transfers: protecting household assets, supporting learning and maintaining people's capacity to work can strengthen recovery and economic participation. For displacement, the opportunity is to connect rapid shock response with continuing access to services as people’s circumstances change, backed by the administrative and financing arrangements needed to make that continuity possible.

8. We need a deal team, not just a coalition of the willing

Country-led investment programs increasingly combine institutional reform, public finance, targeted private investment and credible project preparation. For displacement, this requires developing costed programs with clear budget authority, feasible reforms and implementation capacity. This calls for a multidisciplinary deal team, bringing together protection and sector expertise with public finance, legal, procurement, project preparation and financing capabilities. It also requires political judgment about which institutions can deliver, especially in fragile settings. Some investments can attract private finance; many continuing public obligations will depend on public and concessional resources. The upstream challenge is to translate displacement-related fiscal and institutional requirements into credible, country-owned financing programs.
