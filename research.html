Research Agenda

Governing Displacement

How responsibility for displaced populations is assigned, financed and sustained across institutions and public systems.

Displacement is experienced as a continuum, while governance is organised through fixed legal categories, territorial jurisdictions, institutional mandates and financing arrangements.

The result is often institutional discontinuity. Responsibility, eligibility, data, services and financing fail to move with people as they cross borders, jurisdictions, administrative categories and phases of displacement.

Displacement Policy develops research, comparative evidence and policy frameworks to understand these fault lines — and to identify how responsibility and protection can be sustained more effectively over time.

Analytical Framework

Displacement Policy’s research is organised around three connected analytical lenses: the displacement continuum, sustainable inclusion and political economy.

The Displacement Continuum

Provides a governance diagnostic by identifying where institutional responsibility breaks down as people move across legal categories, jurisdictions, public systems and phases of displacement.

Sustainable Inclusion

Examines how displacement-related functions and access can be sustained through ordinary institutions and public systems where legitimate and feasible, including the institutional, fiscal and administrative arrangements required for continuity.

Political Economy

Examines why institutional discontinuities persist, how interests and incentives shape the terms of inclusion, and what forms of reform are politically, institutionally and fiscally feasible.

Together, these lenses move analysis from identifying where responsibility breaks down to examining what continuity would require and under what conditions reform can be sustained.

Areas of Applied Research

The analytical framework is applied to major public policy questions relevant to displacement. Each research area examines a distinct institutional and policy problem while contributing to the wider Governing Displacement research agenda.

The framework is applicable across different governance systems and forms of displacement. Refugee hosting, conflict-induced internal displacement, disaster and climate mobility, and development-induced displacement create different configurations of institutional responsibility, fiscal capacity, territorial authority and international cost-sharing. This variation provides a basis for examining which governance constraints recur across displacement and which are specific to particular political, institutional or fiscal contexts.

Public Systems and Social Protection

Examining whether ordinary public systems can respond not only to shocks, but to mobility.

Research focuses on how social protection, health, education, civil registration and other public systems can maintain access as people move across jurisdictions, administrative categories and phases of displacement.

Key questions include portability of benefits, eligibility across jurisdictions, interoperable registries, identification, duration-sensitive support and the relationship between humanitarian assistance and national systems.

The wider question is whether systems designed around stable populations and fixed administrative categories can maintain protection and access as people move.

Relevant to: social protection, humanitarian policy, health and education systems, civil registration, digital public infrastructure and public service delivery.

Public Finance and Displacement

Examining how displacement-related responsibilities and expenditure are recognised, allocated and sustained through public and sovereign fiscal systems.

Displacement-related expenditure is often financed as exceptional or temporary even when its consequences become recurrent.

Research focuses on the fiscal architecture underlying displacement: national and subnational budgets, intergovernmental transfers, medium-term expenditure arrangements, contingency financing and other mechanisms through which displacement-related costs and responsibilities are distributed and sustained over time.

A central challenge is the recurrent obligation problem: how expenditure initially financed as temporary or exceptional becomes a continuing public responsibility, and how that responsibility can be reflected in ordinary fiscal systems without assuming that all associated costs must ultimately become domestic.

The research examines how fiscal systems reproduce categorical, temporal and territorial fault lines; how budget and financing arrangements shape incentives for inclusion; and how displacement-related pressures interact with fiscal space and sovereign resilience.

Research also examines how different fiscal and institutional constraints require different financing responses — and how the choice of sector, instrument, financing terms and results should follow from that diagnosis.

A particular focus is the relationship between governance architecture and fiscal architecture: whether resources reach the institutions and jurisdictions carrying responsibility, how costs shift across levels of government, and how fiscal arrangements respond as displacement changes in scale, location or duration.

Relevant to: public finance, fiscal policy, sovereign finance, intergovernmental fiscal systems, development finance, multilateral development banks and humanitarian financing.

Development, Infrastructure and Displacement

Development investments can generate displacement while also shaping the institutions that respond to it.

This area examines how infrastructure finance, environmental and social safeguards and development-bank operations connect project-based displacement responses with longer-term public responsibility.

The focus extends beyond compensation and project compliance to the institutions that sustain responsibility after project closure: public services, social protection, local government, financing and continuing support for affected populations.

A central question is what happens when project obligations end but displacement-related vulnerability continues.

Relevant to: multilateral development banks, infrastructure finance, environmental and social safeguards, development-induced displacement, public investment and country programming.

Climate, Disasters and Mobility

Climate change and disasters increasingly expose the limitations of systems designed around temporary shocks and fixed populations.

This area examines disaster and climate displacement as one application of the wider Governing Displacement framework. Research considers how disaster risk management, adaptive social protection, anticipatory action, planned relocation, adaptation and resilience financing connect with the institutions responsible for people before, during and after movement.

A particular focus is the relationship between emerging legal and policy approaches to avert, minimise and address displacement and the public systems and financing arrangements required to implement them. This includes how responsibilities and expenditure change across emergency response, recovery, relocation and longer-term inclusion; how receiving areas absorb population movement; and how temporary or exceptional interventions connect with sustained institutional responsibility.

The wider question is how climate and disaster governance can address mobility without creating new categorical, territorial or temporal discontinuities.

Relevant to: climate mobility, disaster risk reduction, adaptation, resilience, anticipatory action, planned relocation and loss and damage.

Legal Status, Territory and Multilevel Governance

Examining how responsibility is maintained when displacement crosses legal, territorial and institutional boundaries.

Displacement can move people across legal statuses, administrative jurisdictions, territorial authorities and cross-border frameworks through which responsibility is assigned differently.

Research focuses on how responsibility is allocated and transferred across local, national and regional institutions; how legal protection interacts with administrative systems; and how public functions are maintained as people move between jurisdictions or where authority and territorial reach are fragmented or contested.

The research also examines where sector-specific or administrative forms of inclusion may be possible when comprehensive legal-status reform is politically constrained, and where reliance on ordinary public systems may itself be limited by institutional capacity, protection concerns, legitimacy or territorial control.

The wider question is how responsibility can remain identifiable and workable when the legal, territorial or institutional architecture through which it is normally organised does not align with the realities of displacement.

Relevant to: refugee and IDP protection, migration governance, statelessness, territorial and multilevel governance, local government, regional institutions, legal policy and public administration.

FLAGSHIP RESEARCH PROGRAMME

Financing Displacement Governance

Financing Displacement Governance is a cross-cutting research programme spanning the applied research areas.

Displacement is often framed as a problem of insufficient financing. The underlying challenge is more fundamental: how displacement changes public functions, institutional responsibilities and expenditure, where the resulting fiscal and institutional constraints arise, and what financing is intended to accomplish in response.

The programme examines the wider financing architecture of displacement governance: how responsibilities and costs are distributed across national and subnational governments, public systems, humanitarian actors and international financing; how those arrangements change as people move or displacement becomes prolonged; and how exceptional or externally supported responses connect with ordinary institutions and budgets over time.

The research starts from fiscal and institutional diagnosis rather than from a financing instrument. It asks which public or protection functions are affected by displacement; which institutions actually carry responsibility and expenditure; where fiscal capacity, territorial allocation, institutional rules, volatility or recurrent financing constrain the response; and what combination of institutional change and financing may be required.

The objective is to connect that diagnosis with existing public, development, sovereign, humanitarian, climate and disaster-financing arrangements rather than assume that displacement requires a separate financing architecture. The programme connects public finance with social protection, infrastructure and development investment, climate and disaster financing, humanitarian transitions and multilevel governance, and is designed for application across different displacement settings.

Relevant to: public finance, development finance, sovereign fiscal policy, multilateral development banks, climate and disaster finance, social protection, local government finance and humanitarian financing.

Regional Evidence Base

Southeast Asia Governance Mapping

Displacement Policy’s research agenda is global in scope. Southeast Asia provides its initial regional evidence base and a comparative setting in which to examine broader questions about displacement governance.

The Southeast Asia Governance Mapping examines how countries across the region organise responsibility for displacement through laws, institutions, public systems and policy instruments.

Southeast Asia provides a particularly valuable setting for this work. States combine significant administrative capacity and increasingly sophisticated systems for social protection, disaster management, migration governance and public service delivery with diverse legal and political approaches to refugees, internal displacement, statelessness, disasters and mobility.

The mapping identifies where responsibility is assigned, where it becomes fragmented, and how governance systems respond as people move across legal categories, territorial jurisdictions and phases of displacement.

Its purpose is not simply to catalogue policies. It provides an empirical base for comparative research on institutional continuity, sustainable inclusion, public finance and political economy, while generating propositions that can be examined and developed in other regions.

From Research to Policy Application

Displacement Policy is developing a Displacement Governance Diagnostic that translates the research framework into a structured approach for identifying where institutional continuity breaks down and what would be required to sustain responsibility over time.

Applied work could trace displacement trajectories and the public or protection functions they affect; identify how responsibility is formally assigned and where it is actually exercised; examine the institutional, political-economy, administrative, territorial and fiscal constraints shaping the response; and develop context-specific options for reform. Where financing is a material constraint, analysis could also examine where expenditure and fiscal responsibility fall, how existing financing arrangements affect institutional responses, and what forms of financing may be required to support feasible reform.

The objective is not to prescribe standardised solutions. It is to connect research with practical reform questions:

  • Where does governance fail to follow people?
  • Why do those gaps persist?
  • Where does responsibility actually sit, and which institutions are capable of carrying it?
  • What institutional arrangements or reforms are politically, administratively and fiscally feasible?
  • What resources, financing and forms of responsibility-sharing are required to sustain them?

Depending on the context, applied work may therefore include institutional and system diagnostics, political-economy analysis, fiscal and financing analysis, comparative reform options and structured policy dialogue. These applications also generate evidence through which the wider Governing Displacement framework can be tested and refined.

Such work may be developed with governments, development institutions, international organisations, researchers and practitioners while remaining grounded in Displacement Policy’s independent research agenda.
